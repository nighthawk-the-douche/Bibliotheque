# Metacognition

## <mark style="color:$primary;">Theory</mark>

<mark style="color:$danger;">**Metacognition**</mark> is the **awareness of one's own thought processes and understanding of the patterns behind them**. It is <mark style="color:$primary;">**"thinking about thinking"**</mark>. The term comes from the root word "meta", meaning "beyond" or "above".

### <mark style="color:blue;">What is it?</mark>

A widely cited theory of metacognitive regulation is the Nelson and Narens (1990) model of metacognition. It consists of two levels: the **object level** and the **meta level**:

#### <mark style="color:red;">Cognition</mark>

Also called the <mark style="color:$danger;">**object level**</mark> — this is where cognitive processes or "one's own thinking" takes place. One example is decoding text while reading. At the object level, cognitive strategies (e.g. decoding) are used to help the learner reach a specific goal (understanding the meaning of the text). This is **consciousness**.

#### <mark style="color:red;">Metacognition</mark>

At the <mark style="color:$danger;">**meta level**</mark>, "thinking about thinking" takes place. At this higher-order level, metacognitive strategies are used to ensure the learner is achieving the goal they have set. To continue the reading example, this begins with the learner thinking about how well they understood the paragraph they just read. This is called <mark style="color:$danger;">**monitoring**</mark>. If they are satisfied with their level of understanding, they continue reading. If not, they might reread the paragraph or decide to use a dictionary to help them understand. These actions are called <mark style="color:$danger;">**control processes**</mark>, as they modify the learner's cognitive processes or related behavior based on feedback from monitoring. This is **metaconsciousness**.

### <mark style="color:blue;">The Universality of Metacognition</mark>

<mark style="color:$danger;">**Metacognition**</mark> is a <mark style="color:purple;">**soft skill**</mark> — a skill that affects the <mark style="color:purple;">**fundamental foundations of thinking**</mark>, which makes it simple in theory and difficult in practice. However, this skill is directly linked to **critical thinking, self-analysis, emotional regulation** (since emotions are connected to thoughts), and **learning effectiveness**, all of which together positively affect quality of life.

{% hint style="info" %}
There is no distinction between general and subject-specific metacognitive skills. This means that <mark style="color:$primary;">**metacognitive skills are general in nature**</mark>, and there are no specific skills for certain subject areas. The metacognitive skills used to review an essay are the same as those used to verify an answer to a math question.
{% endhint %}

### <mark style="color:blue;">Research on Students</mark>

A large number of studies have compared students with and without metacognitive skills, for example:

* Metacognitive knowledge can <mark style="color:$primary;">**compensate for IQ**</mark> and <mark style="color:$primary;">**lack of prior knowledge**</mark>. Students with better-developed metacognition used fewer strategies but solved problems **more effectively** than students with poorer metacognition, regardless of IQ or prior knowledge.
* Studies showed that students with the ability to apply metacognitive regulation to their attention and reasoning strategies during mathematics, and then <mark style="color:$primary;">**shift those strategies**</mark> when engaged in science or English literature, were associated with higher academic outcomes in secondary school.
* Students who underwent metacognitive training including pretesting, self-evaluation, and creating study plans performed better on exams. They are self-regulated learners who use the <mark style="color:$primary;">**"right tool for the job"**</mark> and modify their learning strategies and skills based on their awareness of effectiveness.

***

Considering the classic path of a person through life, metacognitive skills and strategy shifting are most effective during school years, as cognitive processes are more flexible at that age than in older people. Additionally, the wide variety of subjects and disciplines at school provides a testing ground for diverse strategies, which is further amplified by a high level of metacognitive awareness and pattern recognition.

{% hint style="warning" %}
It should be noted that metacognitive skills are not universally accurate — **metacognitive bias** exists and is heavily dependent on emotional state, self-esteem, familiarity with the topic, and experience.
{% endhint %}

Metacognition is often considered to have two dimensions: **metacognitive knowledge** and **metacognitive regulation**.

## <mark style="color:$primary;">Metacognitive Knowledge</mark>

Metacognitive knowledge refers to what learners know about learning. This includes:

* <mark style="color:purple;">**The learner's knowledge of their own cognitive abilities**</mark> (e.g. "I have trouble remembering dates in history")
* <mark style="color:purple;">**The learner's knowledge of specific tasks**</mark> (e.g. "The ideas in this chapter I'm about to read are complex")
* <mark style="color:purple;">**The learner's knowledge of different strategies available to them, and when they are appropriate for a task**</mark> (e.g. "If I skim the text first, it will help me understand the overall meaning")

## <mark style="color:$primary;">Metacognitive Regulation</mark>

Metacognitive regulation refers to what learners do about learning. It describes how learners monitor and control their cognitive processes. For example, a learner may realize that a particular strategy is not achieving the desired results, so they decide to try a different one.

### <mark style="color:purple;">Planning</mark>

The planning phase takes place before the task begins. At this stage, learners should ask themselves:

> "What am I being asked to do?"\
> "What strategies will I use?"\
> "Are there any strategies I have used before that might be useful?"

The last question is critical — without it, every task is approached as if for the first time, ignoring all accumulated experience. <mark style="color:purple;">**The quality of planning depends directly on the accuracy of self-assessment**</mark>: a person who overestimates their abilities chooses a strategy that does not suit them, while a person who does not know their limits cannot adapt their approach to real conditions.
{% endstep %}

{% step %}
### <mark style="color:purple;">Monitoring</mark>

The monitoring phase takes place during task execution and is the most demanding and longest phase. During monitoring, learners implement their plan and track the progress they are making toward their learning goal. This involves <mark style="color:$primary;">**tracking attention level**</mark>, <mark style="color:$primary;">**comprehension level**</mark> (rereading when absent), <mark style="color:$primary;">**identifying key elements**</mark>, <mark style="color:$primary;">**maintaining motivation**</mark>, and <mark style="color:$primary;">**checking whether the previously chosen strategy is effective**</mark>. For example, when reading a book, you might easily get distracted and instead of reading consciously, simply stare at the words — when you catch yourself doing this, you reread the block. This process is **similar to meditation**, except that in meditation you simply observe the fact of thinking, while during monitoring you track the state of comprehension and information processing.

If monitoring is absent, a strategy may not be working throughout the entire execution time, and this will only become apparent at the end when nothing can be changed.
{% endstep %}

{% step %}
### <mark style="color:purple;">Evaluating</mark>

During the final evaluation phase, learners determine **how successful the strategy they used was** in helping them achieve their learning goal. To promote evaluation, students can consider:

> "How well did I do?"\
> "What went wrong?"\
> "What could I do differently next time?"\
> "What went well?"\
> "What other types of tasks can I use this strategy for?"
{% endstep %}
{% endstepper %}

## <mark style="color:$primary;">Development</mark> <a href="#rozvitok" id="rozvitok"></a>

To develop metacognitive awareness, the following exercises can be used. Since metacognitive skills are not specific but universal, opportunities to apply and test them can be found in a wide variety of situations.

### <mark style="color:blue;">Media Reflection</mark> <a href="#refleksiya-po-media" id="refleksiya-po-media"></a>

While consuming any media — anime, manga, comics, books, video games, films, series, etc. — track the following factors and ask yourself:

| Task Type                                    | Questions                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| <h4><strong>Attention tracking</strong></h4> | <p>When did I lose focus? What pulled me out? <br>When was I most focused? What caused that?<br>Did I finish or abandon it — and why exactly?</p>                                                                               |
| <h4>Comprehension check</h4>                 | <p>What do I actually remember? <br>Can I explain the plot/structure to someone right now?</p>                                                                                                                                  |
| <h4>Preference analysis</h4>                 | <p>What specifically did I like — visuals, atmosphere, characters, setting, music? <br>What did I not like? Is that a quality problem or a matter of taste? <br>Could I have predicted whether I'd like it before starting?</p> |
| <h4><strong>Transfer potential</strong></h4> | <p>Does anything here connect to what I already know from another genre or type of media? <br>Can any conclusions or lessons from this media be applied to real life?</p>                                                       |

### <mark style="color:blue;">Learn Something New in 30/60 Minutes</mark> <a href="#vivchiti-shos-nove-za-30-60-khvilin" id="vivchiti-shos-nove-za-30-60-khvilin"></a>

Choose any unfamiliar topic.

1. First, write down what you already know, your level of familiarity with the topic, and your intended approach. Before reading, estimate how difficult you expect the topic to be.
2. Stop every 15 minutes and describe in one sentence whether you understand the material and whether the strategy is working.
3. After 60 minutes, check what you actually understood against what you predicted.

### <mark style="color:blue;">Reading Difficult Texts</mark> <a href="#chitannya-skladnikh-tekstiv" id="chitannya-skladnikh-tekstiv"></a>

Find a difficult text on a complex topic — for example, a scientific paper or a philosophy book. Read one page, then write down what you understood, which moments were difficult, and what specifically caused the difficulty. Repeat in cycles.
